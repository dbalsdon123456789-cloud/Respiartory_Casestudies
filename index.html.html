Pathophysiology Link: Why Barbara Is at Risk

COPD is characterized by persistent airflow limitation, chronic inflammation, mucus production, air trapping, and impaired gas exchange. In an acute exacerbation, airway narrowing, bronchospasm, increased secretions, and ventilation-perfusion mismatch increase work of breathing and may cause hypoxemia and hypercapnia.

COPD Treatment and Nursing Considerations

Short-acting bronchodilator: Relieves bronchospasm. Monitor HR, tremor, and response in breath sounds/work of breathing.
Anticholinergic bronchodilator: Reduces bronchoconstriction and mucus effects. Assess dry mouth and inhaler technique.
Systemic corticosteroid: Reduces airway inflammation. Monitor blood glucose, mood changes, infection risk, and sleep disturbance.
Antibiotics: May be ordered when sputum becomes purulent or infection is suspected. Collect cultures if ordered without delaying care.

Bronchial hygiene and breathing strategies

  • Use pursed-lip breathing to prolong exhalation and reduce air trapping.
  • Use SOS for SOB: stop, position, relax shoulders, breathe out slowly through pursed lips, then slow breathing.
  • Encourage effective cough/huff cough if secretions are present.
  • Hydration may help secretion clearance if not contraindicated by heart failure or fluid restriction.

Asthma Pathophysiology Link

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways. Trigger exposure can lead to mast cell activation, inflammatory mediator release, bronchoconstriction, mucosal edema, mucus production, and airway hyperresponsiveness. These changes narrow the airways and cause wheezing, chest tightness, cough, dyspnea, and prolonged expiration.

Trigger or patternClinical meaningNursing implication
Seasonal allergiesAllergic inflammation can worsen airway hyperresponsivenessTeach trigger reduction and controller adherence
Nighttime symptomsSuggests poor asthma controlReview severity classification and action plan
Frequent SABA useRescue inhaler use is increasingAssess control and need for follow-up/controller adjustment
PEF 55%Reduced airflow compared with personal bestTreat acute bronchospasm and reassess
Clinical Pearl: A SABA is a rescue medication. An inhaled corticosteroid is a controller medication and should be used consistently, even when the patient is not actively wheezing.

OSA Pathophysiology and Hospital Safety

Obstructive sleep apnea occurs when the upper airway partially or completely collapses during sleep, causing repeated apnea or hypopnea, intermittent hypoxemia, hypercapnia, arousals, and daytime sleepiness. In hospital, opioids and sedatives can worsen OSA by reducing respiratory drive and airway tone.

Scenario Debrief

Core Clinical Reasoning Takeaways

  • Prioritize patients with severe hypoxemia, altered LOC, reduced RR, silent chest, or rising PaCO₂.
  • In COPD, SpO₂ improvement alone is not enough; ventilation, mental status, pH, and PaCO₂ matter.
  • In asthma, silent chest and rising PaCO₂ are late danger signs.
  • In OSA, opioids and sedatives can worsen airway obstruction and respiratory depression.
  • Focused respiratory assessment includes inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation, tactile fremitus, and voice sounds.
  • Teaching matters after stabilization: inhaler technique, asthma action plans, CPAP adherence, smoking cessation, vaccines, pulmonary rehab, and return precautions.
  • Pharmacology decisions must connect medication purpose, nursing considerations, side effects, and reassessment findings.
  • Lab and diagnostic interpretation should always be connected to nursing implications and anticipated actions.
